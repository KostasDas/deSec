# deSec (Decentralized Security): A Decentralized Protocol Monitoring and Emergency Response Network

**Metana Solidity Bootcamp — Capstone Project Proposal**

---

## Abstract

Smart contract exploits are almost always discovered after the damage is done. Existing monitoring services alert protocol teams that something is wrong, but they are centralized, and they stop at notification. deSec is a decentralized monitoring network in which protocols register on-chain, define an invariant that must always hold for their system, and configure an emergency action — typically pausing their contracts — that executes automatically when that invariant is verifiably broken. Independent watchers are economically incentivized to perform routine invariant checks and to race to trigger the emergency response when a check fails. Because the validity of an invariant check is deterministic and verifiable by the EVM itself, the emergency action is trustless: no watcher, no administrator, and no majority of any kind can cause a pause unless the protocol's own invariant has genuinely failed. This proposal describes the motivation, the contract architecture, the actions available to protocols and to users, and the incentive economy that keeps the network alive.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The Problem

Decentralized finance protocols lose hundreds of millions of dollars to exploits every year, and in the majority of well-known incidents the loss was not instantaneous. Many exploits unfold over multiple transactions, across several blocks, or as slow drains that persist for hours before anyone notices. Detection, when it happens at all, usually comes from a user on social media, a blockchain forensics firm, or the protocol team noticing an anomalous balance — long after the window to limit the damage has closed.

The tools that exist today address only part of this gap. Monitoring platforms such as Tenderly, OpenZeppelin Defender, and Forta can observe on-chain state and raise alerts, but they suffer from one or both of two limitations. First, they are centralized or semi-centralized services, which means the protocol substitutes trust in its code with trust in an operator. Second, and more importantly, they stop at notification. An alert that arrives in a team's inbox at three in the morning does not pause a contract. Between the moment an invariant breaks and the moment a human reacts, the protocol remains fully exposed.

There is also an incentive gap. Even if a protocol knows exactly which condition should never be violated — its solvency ratio, its peg, its collateralization floor — there is no native mechanism for paying strangers to watch that condition continuously and to act the moment it fails.

### 1.2 The Proposed Solution

deSec is a decentralized network that closes both gaps. A protocol registers on-chain and declares three things: an **invariant**, expressed as a function call on its own contracts that must never fail; an **emergency action**, expressed as a privileged function call such as `pause()` that the network is granted the right to invoke; and a **deposit of funds** that pays for the monitoring service. The emergency permission is not granted to the network directly: each protocol deploys its own **GuardianAdapter** — a small, standardized contract from the deSec template, deployed through the network's factory — and grants the emergency permission to that adapter alone. Each protocol's pause power therefore sits in its own isolated contract rather than in a single network contract that holds power over everyone.

Independent users, called **watchers**, monitor registered protocols and submit invariant checks. Registration is anchored to the adapter: the Registry reads the administrator from the adapter's own standardized owner, and a registration only has teeth once the adapter genuinely holds the emergency permission on the protocol's contracts — something only the protocol's own governance can grant. The defining property of the system is that the emergency response is trustless. When a watcher submits a trigger, the Guardian contracts re-execute the invariant check on-chain, at that exact moment, and carry out the emergency action only if the invariant genuinely fails. A watcher therefore cannot cause a false pause, cannot grief a protocol into halting, and cannot fabricate a violation — the EVM itself is the judge of every claim. Watchers are nonetheless paid for their service: small fees for routine checks that confirm the system is healthy, and a substantial bounty for the first valid report of a real violation.

The system is honest about its limits. It cannot stop an exploit that breaks an invariant and extracts value within a single atomic transaction. What it provides is damage limitation: it catches multi-transaction exploits, slow drains, persistent oracle manipulation, and depegging events, and it halts the protocol before a second wave of attack or before queued withdrawals execute. It is equally honest about the emergency action itself: the network cannot guarantee that a pause succeeds, because the permission grant lives with the protocol's own governance and cannot be verified at registration. What the network guarantees is a verified, public, on-chain alarm on every genuine violation — plus a best-effort response whose outcome is recorded on-chain either way.

## 2. Solution Architecture

The system consists of two core contracts plus a small per-protocol adapter, each with a single, well-defined responsibility: the Registry is the vault and the ledger, the Executor is verification and response, and each adapter holds one protocol's emergency permission. Every contract in the system is immutable — there are no proxies and no timelocks anywhere, and the only privileged role in the entire design is a fee recipient whose sole powers are to withdraw the network's own fee pool and to rotate itself; no key can touch a protocol's escrow or logic. This reflects a deliberate principle: any contract that holds pause power over a client protocol, or custody of its funds, must never have its logic silently changed, because an upgradeable guardian would reintroduce exactly the kind of trust in an administrator that the design exists to eliminate. Upgrades happen only through explicit migration, in which a new version is deployed and each protocol chooses to re-register with it.

### 2.1 The DeSecRegistry Contract

The DeSecRegistry is the front door and the record-keeper of the network. Its purpose is to store everything that defines a protocol's relationship with the system.

When a protocol joins, the Registry creates a record containing the exact encoded call of the invariant to be checked — function and arguments baked in together — the exact encoded call of the emergency action to be executed, the economic configuration (the per-check fee, the catch bounty, and the minimum interval between paid checks). Because each call is stored as a complete payload rather than a bare function selector, a single record always represents one precise, pre-authorized interaction: an invariant that asks one exact question such as "is market 4 solvent?", and an emergency action that replays one exact call such as pausing that same market. Watchers who trigger a response can never choose the arguments themselves — they can only fire the call the protocol authorized at registration. In the current version the safety policy is simple and strict: a single verified failure at trigger time is sufficient grounds for the emergency action, because the check is re-executed atomically in the triggering transaction — transient intra-transaction manipulations can never produce a false positive. (A configurable rule requiring the invariant to fail across multiple consecutive blocks, which additionally defends against manipulations that can be sustained for exactly one block, is considered future hardening.)

The Registry does not take the registrant's word for anything. Registration can only be performed by the network's own adapter factory, whose address is set immutably at the Registry's construction — lookalike factories deployed by third parties are rejected outright. Because every protocol joins through this factory-deployed GuardianAdapter, the Registry never has to guess how a given protocol manages access control: each record stores the protocol's adapter, and the Registry reads the adapter's owner — live, at the moment of every privileged call — as the sole authority over the record's safety-critical actions. Governance rotation is therefore nothing more than the adapter's standard two-step ownership transfer: the moment the new owner accepts, the Registry obeys them, with no migration and no stale permissions left behind.

Nothing prevents two records from pointing at the same underlying protocol, and nothing needs to. A record only becomes effective once its adapter genuinely holds the emergency permission on the protocol's contracts — a grant that only the protocol's own governance can make, and that no registrant can fake. Duplicate or fake registrations are therefore inert: they may pay for monitoring out of their own deposits, but they can never fire. Watchers and interfaces identify the live record for a protocol by checking which adapter actually holds the permission.

Registration is further validated by execution. When a record is created, the Registry checks that the invariant target actually holds code and then performs the invariant check itself, once, in read-only mode, requiring a clean true-or-false answer. That single test-fire proves the contract exists, the function exists, it is genuinely read-only, and it returns the expected shape of answer. The network deliberately does not cap the gas of invariant calls: because the invariant is an arbitrary payload whose cost can drift with protocol state, any on-chain cap risks misreading a legitimate-but-expensive check as a violation or bricking a record whose invariant outgrew its allowance. Instead, the cost question is answered where it can be answered honestly — off-chain. Watchers are expected to simulate a check before submitting it, compare the measured gas against the advertised fee, and serve only records whose economics work; a pathological invariant simply goes unserved, which the protocol itself can see and fix by raising its fee.

The invariant function carries one hard requirement: it must be callable by anyone, with no access control of any kind. This is not a convenience but a consequence of the design. Routine checks are performed by anonymous watchers who cannot be known or whitelisted in advance — a permissioned invariant would make permissionless monitoring impossible. Permissions are also impossible to arrange in time: the only network contract a protocol could plausibly whitelist is its GuardianAdapter, and the adapter is deployed during registration itself, so no permission on it can exist when the test-fire happens. Openness costs the protocol nothing, because the invariant is read-only by construction and answers a yes-or-no health question — such as whether collateral covers borrows — whose inputs are already visible to anyone reading the chain. Permissions in this system attach to exactly one thing: the emergency action, which the adapter alone may invoke, and which is only ever needed after registration, once the protocol's governance has had the opportunity to grant the adapter its role.

In the current version, only a record's administrator may fund it — every wei in a record belongs to the protocol itself. This keeps exit accounting simple and safe: no one else's money is ever at stake. The administrator may withdraw funds at any time, down to the reserved bounty — the balance can never drop below the prize while the record exists, so a record cannot quietly drain away its promise while continuing to appear protected. De-registration removes the record and returns everything, and every exit from the network is public. Community funding — allowing anyone to contribute to a protocol's pool — is planned future work. It will require share-based accounting in the style of ERC-4626 vaults, so that refunds at exit are distributed fairly across all contributors rather than flowing to the record's administrator alone.

The Registry also maintains the escrow accounting: each protocol's deposited funds are tracked in an internal mapping keyed by protocol identifier, so that every protocol's money is accounted for separately even though it is held in one contract. Bounty awards are recorded in a separate claimable-balances mapping that exactly one contract may write: the GuardianExecutor, whose address the factory wires into the Registry at deployment through a set-once setter — it can be written exactly once, during the factory's construction, and never changed afterwards. Awarded bounties and earned heartbeat fees wait in the Registry as claimable balances until the watcher withdraws them, so the time-critical trigger transaction never has to push money to an untrusted recipient.

The Registry enforces solvency. A record must always hold its catch bounty in reserve — that is the one hard requirement, enforced at registration and on every configuration change. Fee funding is optional: a protocol may register bounty-only, relying on the prize alone to attract attention, and add a fee drip later with a further deposit. A record may even advertise a fee it cannot currently pay; because every record's funding is public, watchers are expected to check solvency before serving it, and can see at a glance how many checks a record can currently afford. Heartbeat fee payments are additionally blocked from drawing the balance down below the reserved bounty, so that the most important incentive in the system can never be silently consumed by routine operations. Because the remaining-checks count is a free view call, both watchers and the protocol can see the runway shrinking long before it runs out — depletion is never a surprise. When the runway does run out, the record operates in **bounty-only mode**: heartbeats stop paying, but triggers remain fully functional, and the reserved bounty can still be claimed by the first watcher who reports a genuine violation. The administrator may top up the deposit at any time to restore full monitoring. The Registry exposes view functions that allow watchers to inspect current rewards and deposit sufficiency, and only the record's administrator may top up its deposit.

### 2.2 The GuardianExecutor Contract

The GuardianExecutor is the trustless heart of the system. It is the only contract that any protocol's GuardianAdapter will ever obey; protocols grant the emergency permission to their own adapter, never to the Executor directly.

Its trigger entry point accepts a report for a given protocol. When called, it loads the protocol's configuration from the Registry and then performs the invariant check itself, on-chain, via a static call to the function the protocol declared. If the invariant passes, the trigger reverts and nothing happens; the caller has achieved nothing and is owed nothing. Only a clean false verdict counts as a violation: an invariant that reverts is treated as unevaluable rather than breached, and the trigger is rejected — otherwise any crash in the health check could be weaponized into an unearned pause. If the invariant fails, the Executor commands the protocol's GuardianAdapter to invoke the emergency action on the protocol's contracts — immediately, since the failure has just been verified in this very transaction. Finally, it records the catch bounty as claimable by the triggering watcher against that protocol's escrowed deposit.

The bounty is owed for the verified report, not for a successful pause. Registration can never prove that an emergency action will work — the payload cannot be test-fired without firing it, and the adapter's permission is granted only after registration — so the network promises an honest, verified alarm always and a response attempt whose outcome is public. Whether the emergency action succeeds, fails, or was never configured at all, the incident is recorded, the outcome is emitted as an event, and the bounty stands. A protocol may even register with an empty emergency action deliberately, using the network purely as a trustless monitoring service; monitoring-only is not a degraded tier but the honest baseline of every record.

This design means the Executor never trusts the watcher. The watcher is a courier, not a judge: the only thing a watcher can do is draw the Executor's attention to a state that the Executor then verifies independently. Because verification and response happen atomically in a single transaction, a false positive is impossible by construction. The Executor also enforces strict accounting hygiene — bounty payouts follow the checks-effects-interactions pattern, the payout path is protected against reentrancy, and a given incident can pay its bounty only once, which prevents a broken invariant from being triggered repeatedly to drain a deposit.

The Executor also carries routine monitoring. A second entry point accepts a heartbeat check for a protocol: it verifies that the configured interval has elapsed since the last paid check, executes the invariant read-only, and — finding it healthy — records the check time and instructs the Registry to credit the caller with the per-check fee. A heartbeat that discovers a broken invariant does not settle for the fee: it routes into the trigger path, because a watcher who finds a violation has earned the bounty. Because a watcher is an action rather than an identity — any address may check or report — the network needs no staking, enrollment, or watcher-management contract at all. The interval rate-limits rewards, a wasted transaction is its own punishment, and there is no misbehavior a watcher could commit that harms anyone else, so there is nothing to slash and nothing to gate.

### 2.3 The GuardianAdapter Contract

The GuardianAdapter is a small, standardized contract that sits between the network and each client protocol. Every protocol that joins deploys its own adapter through the network's factory — which guarantees that every adapter runs exactly the audited template code — and grants that adapter, and nothing else, the privileged role on its own contracts. The factory and the Registry are bound to each other at deployment: the Registry accepts registrations from no other source, and the factory registers every adapter it deploys, so deployment and registration are a single atomic step.

The adapter has two rules baked in. It knows its owner, set at deployment, and the Registry reads that owner to determine who governs the protocol's record — because the Registry resolves the owner on every call rather than caching it, rotating governance is just the adapter's built-in two-step ownership transfer, and the new administrator's authority takes effect the moment they accept. And it obeys exactly one caller — the GuardianExecutor — so the only path to the protocol's emergency action runs through the Executor's trustless verification.

This indirection buys two properties a direct design cannot offer. The first is isolation: no single contract holds emergency power over every protocol at once, so a flaw in one protocol's setup can never endanger another's. The second is uniformity: the network never has to guess how a given protocol manages access control, because every adapter speaks the same language. Adapters are immutable for the same reason the Executor is — a contract that holds pause power must never have its logic silently changed. An adapter may also hold roles on several of a protocol's contracts at once, allowing an entire system of contracts to be paused together.

### 2.4 Supporting Components

A small set of supporting artifacts completes the architecture. A set of conventions standardizes the expectations between the system and its clients — most importantly the expectation that an invariant is a view or pure call that reads only the protocol's own state, reports health, and can be executed by anyone. An invariant must answer in every state: it never reverts, and degenerate states — an empty market with no borrows, say — count as healthy rather than as exceptions, because a reverting invariant is treated as unevaluable, never as a violation. Invariants may carry arguments baked into the registered payload, so a protocol with parameterized health checks — one per market, say — registers one record per parameter set rather than paying for an on-chain loop over every market on every check; each record remains one exact question with one exact answer. Protocols whose existing contracts are immutable and cannot expose such a function can deploy a small invariant wrapper: a read-only contract that computes the health condition from public state, exposes it as a single permissionless function, and is registered as the invariant target in the core contract's place. Finally, the project includes a deliberately vulnerable mock protocol, a vault whose invariant can be broken on demand, which serves both the test suite and the live demonstration: it allows the entire lifecycle, from registration through violation to automatic pause and bounty payout, to be exercised end to end.

The public API of each contract is defined by a Solidity interface living under `src/interfaces/` — `IDeSecRegistry`, `IGuardianExecutor`, and `IGuardianAdapter` — which declares the contract's functions together with every event and custom error it can produce. Watcher bots, frontends, and integrating contracts build against these interfaces rather than the implementations, so the ABI they code to is a deliberate, stable surface instead of whatever the implementation happens to expose. Each contract inherits its own interface, which makes drift impossible: if an implementation's signature ever stops matching its interface, the code simply does not compile.

## 3. Protocols

### 3.1 What Protocols Gain

A protocol that joins the network gains an automated, always-on safety net that it does not have to operate or staff. Its most critical health condition is checked continuously by economically motivated strangers, and if that condition ever breaks, the protocol pauses itself within the same transaction that confirms the failure — at any hour, with no human in the loop. The protocol also gains credibility with its own users: the registration, the invariant, the deposit, and the entire response policy are public on-chain, so anyone can verify that the protocol has a working dead man's switch and that it is funded. The cost of this protection is transparent and bounded, because the per-check fee and the minimum check interval together determine the maximum rate at which the deposit can be consumed.

### 3.2 How Protocols Incentivize Watchers

Protocols fund the system with an escrowed deposit, denominated in ETH (funding in ERC-20 tokens such as stablecoins is planned future work), and they shape watcher behavior through two incentive tiers. The first tier is the **heartbeat fee**: a small payment, sized to cover the gas cost of a check plus a modest tip, earned by the watcher who performs a routine check once the configured interval has elapsed. The heartbeat fee exists to solve the quiet-days problem — the fact that the overwhelming majority of checks find nothing wrong, and that no watcher would stay online without compensation for them. The second tier is the **catch bounty**: a large payment made to the first watcher whose trigger results in a verified violation — whether or not the emergency action itself succeeds. The asymmetry between the tiers is deliberate. Heartbeat fees keep watchers at their posts at roughly break-even economics, while the bounty is the prize that justifies running serious infrastructure and racing to respond when it matters most. Because the Registry reserves the bounty against the deposit, and because the bounty can only ever be increased while the record exists, a protocol can be confident that the prize it advertises is the prize that will actually be paid — and watchers can be confident it will never be pulled out from under them.

The drip is governed by two protocol-chosen values: the per-check fee and the interval — the minimum time that must pass between paid checks. Everything else is derived rather than stored: the number of checks funded is the unreserved balance divided by the fee, and the remaining runway is that count multiplied by the interval. The interval itself is subject to a network-wide floor, so no configuration can be drained instantly no matter how many watchers compete for its checks. In the current version, all funding comes from the protocol itself; community contributions are planned future work (see section 2.1). The public remaining-checks count therefore serves as the protocol's own renewal reminder.

The network itself takes a small protocol fee — 1% of each awarded bounty, expressed on-chain in basis points (100 out of 10,000) — accruing to a separate accounting pool controlled by the network's fee recipient, so that the infrastructure routing value to watchers can sustain and maintain itself. The fee is skimmed at award time: the advertised bounty is the gross prize, and the watcher's claimable balance is credited the net. Heartbeat drips are not skimmed; only the catch bounty carries the fee, because the bounty is where the meaningful value flows.

Fee payouts follow the same pull pattern as watcher earnings: the accumulated fees wait in the Registry until the fee recipient withdraws them, so the time-critical trigger transaction never pushes money to anyone. The fee recipient is a single address set at deployment to the factory's deployer, and it manages itself without any separate administrator: the current recipient — and only the current recipient — may start a rotation by naming a successor, and the successor must explicitly accept before the role moves. This two-step handover means a mistyped address can never capture the role, because an address that was never intended cannot accept. Any ether sent to the Registry outside a protocol action — whether a deliberate donation or a mistaken transfer — is credited to the network fee pool, since there is no way to identify or refund an anonymous sender; the fee recipient's withdrawal is the only exit for those funds.

### 3.3 Protocol Actions

The protocol owner — realistically a multisig — can perform the following actions, through the factory (registration) and the DeSecRegistry (everything after):

- **Register.** Deploy a GuardianAdapter through the network's factory, declaring the invariant (target contract and an exact encoded call — function and arguments — which must be read-only and callable by anyone), the emergency action (target contract and an exact encoded call — or an empty payload, for protocols that want verified monitoring without granting pause power), the economic configuration, accompanied by an initial deposit that meets the network's minimum registration floor (0.01 ether, applied to both the deposit and the bounty as an anti-grief measure) and covers the bounty plus at least one paid check when a heartbeat fee is declared. Deployment and registration happen in one step, and the invariant is test-fired during the process — arguments included — so malformed configurations never enter the ledger. The Registry reads the administrator from the adapter itself; only that administrator can later perform privileged actions on the record.
- **Deposit.** The administrator may top up the escrow balance at any time to extend the monitoring runway.
- **Withdraw.** Reclaim any funds above the reserved bounty. The balance can never be drawn below the bounty while the record exists; the prize is locked until it is paid to a watcher or reclaimed through de-registration.
- **Set or update the invariant.** Change which condition is monitored as the protocol's own architecture evolves.
- **Set or update the emergency action.** Change which privileged call the Executor will make; in the standard configuration this is the protocol's `pause()` function.
- **Tune parameters.** Adjust the per-check fee and the check interval, subject to the network-wide minimum interval. The catch bounty may only be *increased*, never decreased, while a record exists: a prize that can shrink could be withdrawn in front of a pending trigger, robbing the watcher who raced to report. A protocol that needs a lower bounty must de-register and re-register with a fresh configuration.
- **Grant the guardian role.** On the protocol's own contracts, grant the protocol's GuardianAdapter the privileged role it needs to execute the emergency action. This is the one genuine trust assumption of the system, and it is explicit and revocable.
- **Rotate governance.** Transfer ownership of the adapter to a new administrator — realistically a new multisig — through the adapter's two-step ownership transfer. The Registry reads the owner live from the adapter, so the handover completes the moment the new owner accepts; no separate migration is needed.
- **Resume after an incident.** Unpause or otherwise recover the protocol under its own governance once the cause of a violation has been addressed, and reset the incident flag so the system can respond to future violations.
- **De-register.** Leave the network, revoke the adapter's role on the protocol's contracts, and withdraw all remaining funds, bounty reserve included. A protocol may return at any time by deploying a fresh adapter. (If community funding is introduced later, exit refunds must become contribution-aware first — see section 2.1.)

### 3.4 The Protocol Happy Path

A lending protocol decides to protect itself. Its team defines an invariant — for example, a view function that returns true only while the market's total collateral value covers total borrows — and exposes it on its core contract. The team deploys its GuardianAdapter through the deSec factory, registers with the DeSecRegistry, declaring the invariant, its `pause()` function as the emergency action, a heartbeat fee, a catch bounty, and a check interval of one minute. It funds the escrow with enough ETH to cover the bounty plus ninety days of heartbeat fees, and it grants its adapter the pauser role on its contracts.

From that moment, the protocol is under continuous watch. Once per minute, a watcher performs a check and collects the heartbeat fee; the protocol's deposit depletes slowly and predictably, and the team tops it up during routine operations. Months pass uneventfully. Then, one day, an attacker manipulates the protocol's price oracle, and the solvency invariant breaks. Within seconds, watchers observe the failure; the Executor re-verifies it on-chain at trigger time, confirming it is real and not a transient artifact; the emergency action fires and the protocol pauses before the attacker can complete a second transaction or before queued withdrawals execute. The triggering watcher is paid the bounty from the escrow. The protocol's team investigates, patches the vulnerability, unpauses under its own governance, resets the incident flag, and monitoring resumes. The deposit is lower by one bounty, and the protocol still has its funds.

## 4. Users

### 4.1 Who the Users Are

The user side of the network is open to anyone. In practice, watchers will range from hobbyists running a small script on a home machine to professional infrastructure operators running sophisticated bots, because participation is permissionless and the economic rewards flow to whoever performs the work. The system is designed so that a watcher needs no special relationship with any protocol, no approval, and no identity beyond a funded Ethereum account.

### 4.2 User Actions

A user can perform the following actions:

- **Observe the reward landscape.** Call the Registry's view functions to see, for every registered protocol, the current heartbeat reward, whether the check interval has elapsed, the size of the catch bounty, and whether the protocol's deposit is sufficient to pay. This single off-chain call is what a watcher's bot polls continuously to decide where to direct its attention.
- **Perform heartbeat checks.** Once a protocol's check interval has elapsed, submit a routine check to the GuardianExecutor. It verifies that the interval has passed, executes the invariant, and — finding it healthy — records the check and credits the caller the heartbeat fee, claimable from the Registry. Checks submitted before the interval has elapsed are rejected, so no watcher can drain a deposit by over-checking; the protocol's maximum spend rate is fixed by its own configuration. A check that finds the invariant broken routes into the trigger flow — a watcher who finds a violation has earned the bounty, not the fee.
- **Trigger an emergency response.** When a watcher's off-chain observation shows an invariant has failed, submit a trigger to the GuardianExecutor. The Executor re-verifies the invariant on-chain and, if it truly fails, commands the protocol's GuardianAdapter to execute the emergency action and credits the catch bounty to the watcher's claimable balance.
- **Withdraw earnings.** Claim accumulated heartbeat fees and bounties from the Registry at any time. Earnings are held for withdrawal rather than pushed during time-critical transactions, so that an emergency response is never delayed or made expensive by payments to third parties.
- **Build a track record.** Every check, award, and claim is a public event, so a watcher's history and reliability are reconstructible off-chain — no on-chain registry of identities is needed.

### 4.3 What Happens When a User Correctly Reports a Violation

The correct-report flow is the centerpiece of the system, and it unfolds entirely on-chain in a single transaction. The watcher calls the Executor's trigger function for the protocol in question. The Executor loads the protocol's configuration from the Registry and performs the invariant check itself, through a static call to the protocol's declared function. The invariant fails. Because the check is re-executed inside the triggering transaction itself, the failure is measured against the state of the chain at this exact moment — momentary manipulation inside a single transaction can never produce a false reading here. With the violation confirmed, the Executor commands the protocol's GuardianAdapter to invoke the emergency action, and the protocol pauses in the same transaction — assuming the protocol wired its permissions correctly. If the call fails, the failure is emitted as an event, and neither the alarm nor the bounty is undone.

The Executor then records the incident — setting a flag that prevents any second payout for the same violation until the protocol's owner resets it — credits the catch bounty to the watcher's claimable balance in the Registry, and emits an event announcing the violation, the response, and the reward. The watcher withdraws the bounty at their convenience. Two properties deserve emphasis. First, the watcher could not have caused this outcome fraudulently: if the invariant had actually been healthy, the Executor's own verification would have rejected the trigger, and the watcher would have paid gas for nothing. Second, the protocol's deposit was able to pay the bounty with certainty, because the Registry had reserved it against exactly this eventuality from the day the protocol registered.

## 5. Conclusion

deSec addresses a structural weakness in how the industry defends deployed protocols. The ecosystem has invested heavily in pre-deployment security — audits, formal verification, bug bounties — and in post-hoc forensics, but the space in between, the live window in which an exploit is unfolding, is still guarded mostly by hope and by centralized alerting services that cannot act.

This project demonstrates that the gap can be closed with a small, honest set of contracts. Because the validity of an invariant is something the EVM can verify objectively, the system needs no governance, no voting, and no trusted operator to decide when a protocol is in trouble; verification and response collapse into a single atomic transaction that no participant can abuse. Because watchers are paid for the quiet days as well as for the catastrophic ones, the network sustains the continuous attention that purely bounty-driven systems fail to maintain. And because every element of a protocol's participation — its invariant, its deposit, its response policy — is public and on-chain, the network turns a protocol's operational security posture into something its users can inspect rather than merely trust.

If adopted, systems of this kind would compress the industry's incident response time from hours to seconds for an entire class of exploits, would give smaller protocols access to around-the-clock monitoring they could never staff themselves, and would create a new, open marketplace in which anyone with a machine can earn a living keeping decentralized finance honest. Building it end to end — the registry, the trustless executor, the incentive accounting, the deliberately vulnerable mock protocol, and the test suite that proves the system's guarantees under attack — is a demonstration not only of Solidity engineering, but of the security-first design judgment that the auditing profession runs on.
